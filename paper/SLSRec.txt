Self-Attention Gated Linear Recurrent Units for
Sequential Recommendation
Jian Yang
, Tengfei Bi
, Jiajin Huang
, and Mi Li
, Member, IEEE
Abstract—Sequential recommendation is of utmost importance
for personalized suggestions across various domains, and its
effectiveness hinges critically on accurately capturing the in-
tricate sequential dependencies among items. Two advanced
architectures are widely adopted for this purpose: self-attention
mechanisms (SAM), which excel at modelling global dependencies
in sequences, and linear recurrent units (LRU), which specialize
in capturing local sequential patterns. However, SAM inherently
lacks the capability to convey sequential order information, while
LRU may overemphasize item ordering. Crucially, while the
complementary strengths of SAM and LRU architectures are
promising, their direct integration risks overﬁtting in noisy real-
world recommendation scenarios, as overly reﬁned represen-
tations may predominantly capture training noise rather than
generalizable patterns. To tackle this issue while preserving the
advantages of both architectures, we design a dual-pronged
solution: perturbation injection during recurrence enhances
robustness, while Gibbs distribution-based uncertainty scaling
reduces noise sensitivity. Integrating these strategies, we propose
a self-attention gated linear recurrent units for sequential rec-
ommendation (SLSRec) model. Speciﬁcally, through a novel self-
attention gated unit, SLSRec synergistically integrates SAM’s
global modeling capability with LRU’s sequential processing
strength. The model is optimized using a comprehensive objec-
tive function that combines cross-entropy recommendation loss,
perturbation injection loss, and uncertainty-incorporated recom-
mendation loss. Extensive experiments on real-world datasets
demonstrate that SLSRec outperforms state-of-the-art methods,
showing particular effectiveness in handling noisy interactions
and recommending long-tail items.
Index Terms—Linear recurrent unit (LRU), perturbation in-
jection, self-attention mechanism (SAM), sequential recommen-
dation, uncertainty scaling.

I. INTRODUCTION
R
ECOMMENDER systems occupy a pivotal position in
various internet-based application domains, notably e-
commerce platforms [1], social networks [2], [3], video stream-
ing services [4] and music applications [5], by tailoring per-
sonalized suggestions to users’ preferences. Among these so-
phisticated systems, sequential recommendation stands out as
a cornerstone, attracting considerable research attention for its
ability in predicting users’ subsequent actions based on their
historical behavioral sequences. The evolution of sequential
recommendation has been marked by a transition from classical
Markov chain methods [6], [7] to advanced deep learning archi-
tectures, in particular recurrent neural networks (RNN) [8], [9],
[10] and convolutional neural networks (CNN) [11], [12], which
have revolutionized the landscape of sequence modelling.
In recent years, the Transformer architecture has revitalized
sequential recommendation and has become one of the main-
stream techniques for accurately delivering personalized and
high-quality recommendations to users. Under the architecture,
self-attention mechanism (SAM)-based models [13], [14] skill-
fully construct a self-attention matrix by deeply mining the
inherent rules of transaction sequence data, which not only
accurately captures the complex dependencies and relative im-
portance among items in the sequence, but also promotes a
signiﬁcant improvement in recommendation accuracy. Building
upon this line of research, newer approaches like ScaleRec
[15] further reﬁne the capability of SAM to capture patterns
at different granularities. By extending the architecture of the
Transformer, linear recurrent units (LRU)-based models [16],
[17] have shown remarkable potential and broad prospects in
sequential recommendation by ﬁnely analyzing the sequential
logic of items in the transaction sequence and revealing their
dynamic dependency relationships. More recently, architectures
like SIGMA [18] have explored selective gating mechanisms
within state-space models, demonstrating another promising di-
rection for efﬁcient and effective sequential modeling. Although
SAM-based models and LRU-based models have demonstrated
excellent performance in the area of sequential recommenda-
tion, they both face a common challenge: How to appropriately
capture the order relationship of items in users’ historical
transaction sequences?

among items in a sequence are very important, while others
are relatively unimportant [19]. For an illustrative example,
consider user Janet’s purchase sequence: {iPhone, earphones,

SAM-based models adopt a global perspective to assign

the subsequent one, enabling them to accurately sequence four
items related to mobile phones. However, this frequently leads
to recommendations that are narrowly conﬁned to earphones
after an iPhone purchase, neglecting chargers and data cables.
Ultimately, due to their inherent architectural constraints, nei-
ther SAM nor LRU can effectively strike a balance in capturing
item order information.
A direct and effective approach to enhancing sequential rec-
ommendation systems lies in combining SAM with LRU. Re-
cent efforts have explored similar synergistic designs; for in-
stance, BiSGR-Att [21] integrates bidirectional gated recurrent
networks with linear attention to capture sequential dependen-
cies from dual perspectives. While this integration leverages
SAM’s ability to capture intricate cross-item patterns along-
side LRU’s proﬁciency in modeling local temporal transitions,
it introduces an inherent risk of amplifying model complex-
ity. Other contemporary approaches address related challenges,
such as enhancing explainability through disentangled repre-
sentations [22] or separating long-term and short-term user
interests for debiasing [23]. These works highlight the multi-
faceted nature of improving sequential recommenders beyond
mere accuracy. When these mechanisms are merged without
careful calibration, the resulting model may generate overly
reﬁned representations that overﬁt both meaningful patterns
and training data noise, ultimately degrading generalization
performance. This challenge is particularly pronounced in real-
world recommendation systems that rely on implicit feedback,
where noisy interactions and mislabeled items are prevalent,
as highlighted in [24]. Exploring alternative paradigms, such
as frequency-domain ﬁltering [25], [26], also represents an
ongoing effort to build more robust sequence representations.
The overﬁtting phenomenon arises from two interrelated fac-
tors. First, the quadratic computational complexity of SAM
and the linear parameter growth of LRU jointly create a high-
capacity model that is prone to memorizing spurious correla-
tions in limited training data. Second, implicit feedback of-
ten contains random behaviors or misclicks; SAM’s global
attention may inadvertently assign excessive weight to these
noisy interactions, while LRU’s sequential updates could prop-
agate such errors through hidden states, compounding their
impact. These conﬂicting optimization goals result in unstable
parameter updates, further exacerbating overﬁtting. Empirical
evidence from Section IV-C (Fig. 3) demonstrates that direct
fusion of SAM and LRU signiﬁcantly ampliﬁes overﬁtting to
training data, including noise, thereby reducing performance on
validation sets.
To effectively mitigate the risk of overﬁtting associated with
the fusion of SAM and LRU, we adopt a dual-pronged strat-
egy. On one hand, we enhance the robustness of the model
by perturbing its recurrent process. This is achieved by ran-
domly substituting an item representation in each layer with an
embedding drawn from a uniform distribution. Such random
perturbation is intended to mitigate overﬁtting resulting from
noisy data, thereby improving the model’s robustness. On the
other hand, we augment the output ﬂexibility by incorporating
uncertainty scaling factors into the prediction outcomes. Un-
certainty scaling, which is grounded in a Gibbs distribution,
serves as a probabilistic regularization technique to alleviate the
impact of inherent noise on the predicted values.
Based on this idea, we propose a self-attention gated lin-
ear recurrent units for sequential recommendation (SLSRec)
model, which combines SAM and LRU into parallel branches
through gating mechanisms, thereby complementing their re-
spective advantages. SLSRec inherits the parallel efﬁciency
of SAM, which concurrently assigns attention weights to all
items, and the sequential capability of LRU, which systemat-
ically traverses sequences step-by-step. Furthermore, SLSRec
mitigates overﬁtting caused by the fusion of SAM and LRU
through a dual-pronged strategy: perturbation injection and un-
certainty scaling techniques. By executing the LRU and SAM
components in parallel and integrating them via elementwise
multiplication and augmented with the anti-overﬁtting strat-
egy, the proposed SLSRec model effectively captures intri-
cate sequential dependencies while ensuring robustness against
overﬁtting.
In summary, the ﬁrst challenge addressed in this article is
how to appropriately capture the order relationship of items
in users’ historical interaction sequences. Our solution is to
integrate SAM and LRU through a gating mechanism (see the
model architecture of SLSRec in Fig. 1). Overall performance
in Section IV-B and ablation experiments in Section IV-C vali-
dates that this integration outperforms using either model alone.
The second challenge we confront is how to mitigate the overﬁt-
ting risk caused by noisy interactions during model integration.
Our strategy is to employ perturbation injection and uncertainty
scaling during model training (see the model training diagram
of SLSRec in Fig. 2). Both ablation experiments in Section IV-C
and noise and long-tail scenarios experiments in Section IV-D
demonstrate the effectiveness of these techniques.
The main contributions of this article are as follows.
1) In terms of model architecture, we propose to integrate
SAM and LRU to balance item order considerations.
Using SAM as a gating signal and combining it into the
iterative LRU process, we enable the LRU to fully capture
sequential information.
2) In terms of model training, we propose to com-
bine perturbation injection and aleatoric uncertainty
strategies to improve recommendation performance.
We develop sophisticated uncertainty scaling to as-
sess uncertainty from both sequence and target item
representations.

II. RELATED WORK
A. Sequential Recommendation
In the ﬁeld of sequential recommendation, a series of innova-
tive advances have dramatically changed the landscape of per-
sonalized recommendations. Early efforts focus on exploiting
the synergy between Markov chain [6] and matrix factorization
techniques [7], meticulously building models that capture user
preferences rooted in their historical interaction patterns. MF
[27], FISM [28] and FPMC [7] are typical representatives of
matrix factorization techniques in the ﬁeld of recommendation
systems. FISM combines factorization techniques, while FPMC
integrates Markov chain methods. Together, these methods ex-
plore innovative technological frameworks that lay the ground-
work for subsequent advances in sequential recommendation.
The advent of deep learning methods [29] represents a paradigm
shift, ushering in a new era of increased expressiveness and ca-
pability for sequential recommendation. Pioneering the fusion
of neural networks with sequential recommendation, CNN [19],
[30], RNN [8], [9], [10] and Transformers [31], open up new
avenues for unravelling the complexity of sequential data. For
example, GRU4Rec [8], [9] and NARM [32] are recommen-
dation algorithms carefully designed on the RNN architecture,
which are early attempts to fuse deep learning paradigms with
the frameworks of recommender systems.
Transformer-based architectures [13], [33] leverage their so-
phisticated SAM to intricately identify and exploit relation-
ships among items, resulting in signiﬁcant gains in prediction
accuracy. SASRec [13], an exemplary model in the ﬁeld of
Transformer-based architectures, achieves remarkable recom-
mendation performance, thereby establishing a benchmark in
the ﬁeld of sequential recommendation. BERT4Rec [33] in-
novates by incorporating a bidirectional encoding architecture
where the historical sequence of user interactions is concep-
tualized as a form of textual data. ScaleRec [15] captures
item correlations at various scales, further enhancing the repre-
sentational power of attention-based methods. In addition, re-
search shows that multi-layer perceptron (MLP)-based models,
in particular FMLP-Rec [25], adeptly balance prediction accu-
racy with computational efﬁciency. Exploring new dimensions,
WEARec [26] utilizes wavelet-enhanced adaptive frequency
ﬁlters to learn representations, offering a different perspective
on modeling sequential patterns.
More recently, the revitalization of LRU [16], ﬁrmly
grounded in the robust architecture of state space models [34],
marks a signiﬁcant leap forward. Using innovative techniques
of linearization, diagonalization, stable exponential parameter-
ization [35], [36], [37] and tailored initialization strategies,
LRU becomes the cornerstone of powerful and efﬁcient state
space-based models. LRURec [16] pioneers the integration of
LRU into sequential recommendation and sets the benchmark
for LRU-driven recommendations. Building on this success,
RecBLR [17] extends the concept by integrating real-valued
LRU with gating mechanisms, thereby enhancing their adapt-
ability to dynamic user behavior and achieving overall per-
formance improvements. Further advancing this line of work,
SIGMA [18] introduces selective gating within the Mamba
architecture, demonstrating the potential of combining state-
space models with sophisticated gating mechanisms for sequen-
tial recommendation.
SAM-based models inherently do not capture the temporal
properties of items in the sequence well (although they intro-
duce item positions into the embedding) [20], while LRU-based

fusing SAM and an LRU architecture. Our work shares a similar
goal with models like BiSGR-Att [21], which also attempts to
synergize gated recurrent networks with attention mechanisms.
However, our proposed SLSRec distinguishes itself through

Addressing inherent noise in data has always been one of
the key concerns in sequential recommendation. For example,
FMLP-Rec [25] uses a learnable ﬁlter to remove noise from the
sequence, and unlike the previous explicit denoising method,
STOSA [38] uses a probabilistic embedding layer to represent
each item, which indirectly improves the robustness of the
model to noise. Relatedly, improving the quality of represen-
tations has been tackled from different angles, such as learning
disentangled representations for better explainability [22] or
separating long- and short-term interests to reduce bias [23].
Although denoising is beyond the scope of this article, the
perturbation injection approach we use has a similar idea to
STOSA. The goal of perturbation injection is not to identify
and remove noise, but to introduce disturbances within the
model to reduce over-reliance on the noise inherently present
in the interaction data (e.g., as described in [39]), i.e., it is
used as a regularization technique to improve the stability of
the model.
B. Uncertainty Estimation in Recommender Systems
The notions of epistemic uncertainty and aleatoric uncer-
tainty, originally introduced in computer vision [24], [40], have
subsequently been adapted and applied to address real-world
challenges [41], [42]. Sequential recommendation also faces
uncertainty, and considerable efforts have been made to model
the two fundamental types of uncertainty inherent in recom-
mender systems.
One technology roadmap focuses on epistemic uncertainty,
where most studies tackle the cold-start problem to generate dif-
ferent recommendations [43], [44]. For example, STOSA [38]
employs Gaussian embedding, which incorporates randomness
into embeddings, to dynamically capture the uncertain prefer-
ences displayed by certain users. Another stream of research
focuses on modelling aleatoric uncertainty from a data-centric
perspective. For example, AUR [45] focuses on a single task
and leverages a Gaussian process-based uncertainty estimator
to generate recommendations while accounting for mislabeling
issues. In addition, Kendall et al. address multitask scenar-
ios, utilizing uncertainty to assess the importance of different
tasks [46]. BDL [47] integrates both epistemic uncertainty and
aleatoric uncertainty into a reinforcement learning-based rec-
ommendation framework, aiming to optimize the exploration-
exploitation balance while incorporating model predictions and
uncertainties. In the context of knowledge distillation, UKD
[48] introduces aleatoric uncertainty to mitigate the effect of
inherent noise in pseudo-labels.
Our work follows the data driven approach, but tackles the
inherent noise challenge speciﬁc to implicit sequential recom-
mendations. We model the prediction value as a Gibbs distri-
bution, which provides a novel perspective to address this issue
in sequential recommendation.
III. METHODOLOGY
Previous studies have shown that SAM and LRU can effec-
tively capture sequential dependencies, respectively. However,
they have inherent limitations in either ignoring or overempha-
sizing the importance of item order information in a sequence,
which limits their recommendation performance. In this sec-
tion, we present how to integrate SAM and LRU to adaptively
utilize the item order in a sequence, and how to improve the
robustness and long-tail effectiveness of the model by using
perturbation injection and aleatoric uncertainty techniques.
A. Problem Description
In sequential recommendation, let U denote the user set, I
denote the item set, and Su = {v1, v2, · · · , vN|vt ∈I, 1 ≤t ≤
N} be the chronologically ordered interaction sequence for user
u ∈U, where N is the maximum sequence length. Given the
interaction sequence Su, the task of sequential recommendation
is to predict the next item to interact with user u. Typically, the
task is transformed into computing the probabilities of user u
interacting with all candidate items and recommending the top-
K items with the highest probabilities to the user.
B. Model Architecture
The overall architectural design of our proposed SLSRec
model is illustrated in Fig. 1. It comprises an embedding layer,
a modelling layer and a prediction layer. The modelling layer
is its core part and consists of stacked L recurrent layers each
of which has two modules: a self-attention gated linear recur-
rent unit (SG-LRU) and a position-wise feed-forward network
(PFFN). SG-LRU is our creative module that integrates SAM
and LRU to comprehensively capture the sequential relation-
ships among items in the sequence.

IV. EXPERIMENT AND ANALYSIS
To validate the performance of our SLSRec model, we con-
duct several experiments on ﬁve real-world datasets. Our objec-
tives are to compare its performance with twelve state-of-the-
art baseline models and to perform a thorough model analysis.
In essence, we attempt to answer the following four research
questions (RQs).
1) RQ1: How does SLSRec perform against various out-
standing baselines?
2) RQ2: How do the key modules in SLSRec affect its
overall performance?
3) RQ3: How does SLSRec perform on noisy interactions
or long-tail items?
4) RQ4: How do hyperparameters affect the performance of
SLSRec?

A. Experimental Setup
1) Datasets: We implement experiments and evaluate our
model using the following ﬁve real-world datasets.
a) MovieLens-1M: A benchmark dataset commonly used
for movie recommendation tasks. We utilize the widely
adopted ML-1M version here [54].
b) Amazon: A collection of datasets featuring product re-
views sourced from Amazon. Speciﬁcally, we select the
Beauty, Video and Sports categories for our analysis [55],
[56].
c) Steam: A dataset of video game reviews obtained from
Steam, a prominent online video game distribution plat-
form [13].
For each dataset, interaction records are grouped by user,
and then sorted by ascending timestamp to form the interaction
sequence for each user. To reduce the workload of the exper-
iment and to ensure a fair comparison, we closely follow the
conﬁgurations outlined in the LRURec work [16] and request
experimental datasets directly from the authors. In particular,
these datasets are preprocessed using the procedures outlined
in [13], [14], [57], [58] to construct input sequences. Users
and items with fewer than ﬁve interactions are excluded, and
the maximum sequence length is set to 200 for ML-1M and
50 for the other four datasets. These datasets are partitioned
using the leave-last-out strategy, where the most recent item is
assigned to the test set, the next most recent item is assigned to
the validation set, and all other items in the sequences together
form the training set. During the testing phase, both training and
validation items are included as part of the input. The statistics
of these datasets after preprocessing are shown in Table I.
2) Baseline Methods: We evaluate the performance of our
SLSRec model by benchmarking it against a comprehensive
set of baseline methods, including traditional factorization and
Markov chain techniques (MF, FISM and FPMC), RNN-based
architectures (GRU4Rec and NARM), Transformer-based or
MLP-based models (SASRec, BERT4Rec and FMLP-Rec), an
uncertainty aware method (STOSA) and LRU-based models
(LRURec and RecBLR). A brief overview of these methods is
given below.
a) MF [27]: A classical factorization model that infers latent
representations for users and items to predict next-item
interactions.
b) FISM [28]: Rather than explicitly modelling user prefer-
ences, FISM predicts subsequent interactions by exploit-
ing item-to-item similarity.
c) FPMC [7]: A matrix factorization model that employs
Markov chains to capture user transition patterns.

3) Evaluation Metrics: We use two standard metrics, Re-
call (Recall@K) and normalized discounted cumulative gain
(NDCG@K) with K ∈{10, 20}, to evaluate the performance
of a model. For unbiased evaluation, we rank predicted items
relative to all items in the dataset to calculate the metric
scores.
4) Implementation Details:
For all baseline models, we
strictly follow the protocols detailed in the original publications
for their respective implementations [16], [17], [38]. All of them
except for STOSA are implemented using the LRURec code
repository, with their parameters conﬁgured identically to those
in LRURec [16], [17]. Speciﬁcally, each model is trained using
the cross-entropy loss function, employing the AdamW opti-
mizer with a constant learning rate of 1e-3 and a weight decay
of 1e-2. During the training process, the embedding dimension
remains ﬁxed at 64, a batch size of 128 is utilized, and training is
limited to a maximum of 1000 epochs. Validation is conducted
every 5 epochs, and an early stopping mechanism is activated if
there is no improvement in the validation Recall@10 metric for
10 consecutive validation epochs. The dropout rate is selected
from the set {0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8}.
For STOSA, to make a fair comparison, we follow its original
parameter selection strategy. The embedding dimension, the
maximum sequence length, the learning rate, the L2 regular-
ization weight and the dropout rate are chosen from {32, 64},
{50, 200}, {1e-4, 1e-3}, {1e-3, 1e-2, 1e-1} and {0.3, 0.5, 0.7},
respectively.
For our SLSRec model, its implementation is also based
on the LRURec code repository. All common parameters are
conﬁgured identically to the above baselines, except for the
dropout rate. In response to the complex characteristics of the
various datasets, we adopt a more comprehensive hyperparam-
eter search strategy for the dropout rate. Speciﬁcally, we tune it
in {0, 0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8} for ML-1M, Steam and Video, and in
{0, 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, 0.95} for Beauty and Sports, respectively.
This approach allows us to tailor the dropout rate to the unique
characteristics of each dataset, thereby improving the model’s
performance across different data distributions. The model spe-
ciﬁc hyperparameters of SLSRec, the perturbation rate θ and the
uncertainty parameter α, are chosen from {0.05, 0.1, 0.15, 0.2,
0.25} and {0.01, 0.05, 0.1, 0.3, 0.5, 0.7, 1}, respectively.
B. Overall Performance (RQ1)
As the experimental datasets, including their training/
validation/test segmentation, are the same as those used in [16],
[17], we directly use the reported results of these studies for
comparison. Our primary results are summarized in Table II,
where the best results are bolded and the second-best results
are underlined for clarity. Additionally, we calculate the relative
improvement of our proposed SLSRec over the best performing
baseline, denoted as “Improv.” in Table II. From the table, we
can make the following observations.
1) Traditional methods exhibit comparatively suboptimal
performance. MF, FISM, and FPMC rely on matrix de-
composition or Markov chains to capture sequential pat-
terns, which are limited to capturing only short-term de-
pendencies, ignoring long-term dependencies. They are
therefore limited to identifying only low-order, simple
dependencies.
2) RNN-based methods outperform traditional models in
performance. In particular, GRU4Rec and NARM model
sequences by progressively updating their hidden states,
and uniquely incorporate a gating mechanism that enables
them to capture longer dependencies compared to FISM
and FPMC.
3) Transformer-based or MLP-based methods beat RNN-
based models. SASRec and BERT4Rec use SAMs and
FMLP-Rec uses a fully connected MLP layer to cap-
ture sequence interactions, and they capture global in-
formation and emphasize those interactions that are truly
relevant. Remarkably, integrating a frequency ﬁlter with
SAMs enables BSARec to secure the second-best perfor-
mance on the Sports dataset, highlighting the signiﬁcant
impact of combining SAMs with other models in sequen-
tial recommendation.
4) LRU-based methods outperform previous Transformer-
based and MLP-based models. LRURec and RecBLR use
state space model-based LRU to model sequences, ensur-
ing efﬁcient parallelization of both training and inference.
In addition, they incorporate residual connections and
PFFNs to further enhance the representation capabilities
of these models.

C. Ablation Study (RQ2)
The main contributions of SLSRec lie in the seamless in-
tegration of a self-attention component and an LRU compo-
nent, coupled with perturbation injection and uncertainty scal-
ing techniques, which together facilitate effective learning of
appropriate integration. To investigate the speciﬁc impact of
these components on the performance of SLSRec, we conduct a
comprehensive ablation study. In this study, customized variants
of SLSRec, each with a different modiﬁcation, are evaluated:
w/o SAM excludes the self-attention layer within the SG-LRU
module, w/o BD-LRU removes the BD-LRU layer from the
SG-LRU module, w/o P neglects the perturbation injection
task, w/o U omits the uncertainty scaling task, and w/o P&U
simultaneously removes perturbation injection and uncertainty
scaling tasks, i.e., it is the simple combination of SAM and
LRU. The comparative performance of SLSRec and its modi-
ﬁed variants is shown in Table III. From this table, the following
observations can be made.
1) Each component plays a critical role in boosting the
overall effectiveness of SLSRec, with one exception: w/o
BD-LRU produces an increase in NDCG@20 for Video.
In contrast, the removal of any other component leads to
a decrease in performance across different datasets, albeit
to varying degrees.
2) The inﬂuence of individual components varies depending
on the speciﬁc characteristics of the dataset. A notable
observation is the signiﬁcant average performance drop of

E. Hyperparameter Sensitivity (RQ4)
We perform an extensive series of hyperparameter experi-
ments on ﬁve different datasets to assess the sensitivity of our
SLSRec model to variations in dropout rate, perturbation rate,
and uncertainty parameter. The purpose of these experiments
is to explore the optimal conﬁguration of these hyperparam-
eters and to gain insight into their positive impact on model
performance.
1) Dropout Rate: The dropout rate controls how many neu-
rons in the neural network are randomly discarded, reducing
the model’s dependence on speciﬁc training samples and thus
reducing the risk of overﬁtting. This forces the network to learn
more robust features that generalize better across different data
samples. We conduct experiments with different dropout rates
across multiple datasets. Figs. 6 and 7 illustrate a comparison of
the model’s performance under different hyperparameter con-
ﬁgurations. From these ﬁgures, we can see that the optimal per-
formance is achieved at a dropout rate of 0.2 for dense datasets,
such as ML-1M, and at a dropout rate of 0.75 for sparse datasets,
such as Sports. This suggests that the appropriate dropout rate
is dataset dependent and that ﬁne-tuning this hyperparameter is
essential to maximize the effectiveness of the model.

trols the amount of perturbation introduced into the training
set during the training phase. We ﬁne-tune it within a range
of 0.05 to 0.25 to investigate its effect on the performance
of SLSRec. As shown in Table V, the performance of the
SLSRec shows a noticeable trend as the hyperparameter θ
varies. In particular, the optimal performance is observed at

V. CONCLUSION
To properly exploit item order information within historical
interaction sequences, we propose a sequential recommenda-
tion model based on self-attention gated LRU, called SLSRec.
In terms of its model structure, SLSRec integrates different
modelling techniques, using SAMs and LRUs in a carefully
crafted manner. In terms of technical skill, SLSRec incorpo-
rates perturbation injection and uncertainty scaling strategies
to tackle the pervasive problem of noisy data in recommender
systems. By synergizing its sophisticated model structure with
advanced technical skills, SLSRec excels at capturing sequen-
tial dependencies, thereby generating highly accurate recom-
mendations. It also demonstrates robustness in mitigating the
impact of noisy transactions and in dealing with the long-tail
effect. Extensive experiments conducted on real-world datasets
underscore SLSRec’s superior recommendation performance,
outperforming a number of state-of-the-art baseline models that
are based on Transformer, LRU and uncertainty modelling.
Future research could focus on exploring more strategies for
exploiting sequential relationships, such as direct modelling or
the concatenation of SAM and LRU. This could allow a more
reﬁned use of the item order information and further improve
the model’s recommendation performance. Another promising
direction of investigation would be to develop a more efﬁcient
uncertainty estimator and combine it with contrastive learning,
to further improve the long-tail effectiveness and robustness of
the model.
